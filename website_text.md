Infant Relinquishment: The Untold History
	During the 1960s hundreds of thousands of women, mostly young and unmarried, relinquished their infants to adoption and social service agencies. These women were shamed, silenced, and isolated. This important part of history remains untold and unshared today. It is time to change this NOW … failed practices of the past echo today in our sons and daughters and in current processes of adoption and assisted reproduction.  The women who can tell the story are dying off. We need your help NOW to make our voices heard.   
WE are the women of Infant Relinquishment: The Untold History.
The mission of Infant Relinquishment: The Untold History is to create an archive of our experiences to preserve this chapter in history and to provide data for advocating for legal and social change.  This archive may be used for a book for the general public or as a tool for research.

About page		
	This project was conceived by a group of women with years of experience in support groups, adoption reform advocacy groups and birthparent organizations. This history remains untold on purpose…secrecy and silence were the guiding principles of our experience, and history is most often written by the men who often were erased from our stories. The era of closed adoptions has largely come to an end, and those that can testify to the experience are dying off. While closed adoptions may be on the decline, some of the practices – convincing women they are unworthy to parent, signing legal documents without informed consent or legal representation, for instance - are not.  

This is an ambitious project, and it must be done now and by you.  Those who relinquished infants – mothers and fathers – are the only ones who can and will tell the story.

Do You Remember?
I relinquished my baby in 1966, at the height of the Baby Scoop Era. An essential part of my healing is revisiting those days to understand who I was and why I made the decisions I did. 
 
I graduated from high school in June,1965. 
	Our tinny transistor radios played tunes of male domination:  	 
	 	I Will Follow Him 
Leader of the Pack 
And meek female independence 
It’s My Party and I’ll Cry if I Want To. 
Girls (and we were girls) could aspire to be a nurse, secretary, or teacher, until they were married, had children, and became homemakers 	 	 
Military recruiters were welcome at school with promises of adventure and Hawaii. Vietnam was not mentioned. Only boys were welcome. 
Sex Education was abstinence. Girls were to be coyly  flirtatious daring enough to keep a boy interested but not to go all the way: “Boys will be boys” and therefore bore no sexual responsibility. 
All my classmates were raised in a two-parent household unless a mother was widowed. I never met or knew a single mom. Divorce was something Hollywood did. 
LBJ’s Great Society was in the future. Social welfare programs were largely the purview of religious organizations. 
The vast majority of American families attended church and relied on religious leaders for spiritual guidance, 
In Massachusetts, where I attended college, contraception for unmarried women and abortion was illegal.  
No one in my family had ever attended college. I was white, middle-class, suburban, Irish, and Catholic. 
Unwed mothers chewed gum, had bleached blonde hair, hung out with bad boys and were featured in trashy magazines.  

DO YOU REMEMBER ? 
In 1966 I had consensual sexual intercourse when I was unmarried. I was delivered of a daughter, and signed papers of relinquishment to the Catholic Home Society of New York. These were my actions, and I am responsible for them and all the consequences. 
Bob Dylan told us “The Times They are ‘AChangin’ “.
Social protest against the Vietnam War, the “establishment” and for the rights of women dominated the headlines in 1968. 
Food stamps were made available in all states. 
Contraception bans were overturned in the courts, culminating in Roe v Wade in 1973. 
The Equal Rights Amendment was introduced in Congress in 1971 
Candice Bergen was Murphy Brown, a single mother by choice 
Women were increasingly admitted to law, medical and graduate professional programs 
Erica Jong’s Fear of Flying educated the public about female sexuality.
  Our stereos blasted a new soundtrack: 
	 	I am Woman, Hear Me Roar! 
	 	Go Where You Want to Go, Do What You Want to Do 
	 	Respect 
 
I never became a nurse, teacher, or secretary. Instead, I graduated cum laude with a major in history and a minor in philosophy, then built a long, successful career in information technology and insurance management. And I never chew gum. 
 	 
Eileen McQuade 	July 20, 2026 	Page 2

18.	Were you given anything to ease pain? What?
19.	Did you see you baby in the delivery room?

22.	When did you sign relinquishment documents (how soon after delivery?)
23.	Did you understand what the documents meant?
24.	Were you told about revocation?
25.	Did you have your own legal representation?
26.	Were you offered support or services after discharge from hospital?
27.	Describe how you felt when you were discharged from the hospital?
28.	How would you describe your feelings and behaviors a year after relinquishment?
29.	After 5 years? 10 years? Now?
30.	Who have your shared your story with?
31.	Have you sought help or support from a therapist or support group?
32.	How did the experience of relinquishment effect the woman you are today?
