Infant Relinquishment: The Untold History

During the 1960s hundreds of thousands of women, mostly young and unmarried, relinquished their infants to adoption and social service agencies. These women were shamed, silenced, and isolated. This important part of history remains untold and unshared today. It is time to change this NOW … failed practices of the past echo today in our sons and daughters and in current processes of adoption and assisted reproduction. The women who can tell the story are dying off.

We need your help NOW to make our voices heard.

Our Mission

The mission of Infant Relinquishment: The Untold Herstory is to create an archive of our experiences to preserve this chapter in history and to provide data for advocating for legal and social change. This archive may be used for a book for the general public or as a tool for research.

About

Preserving a history that was hidden by design

This project was conceived by a group of women with years of experience in support groups, adoption reform advocacy groups and birthparent organizations.

This history remains untold on purpose…secrecy and silence were the guiding principles of our experience, and history is most often written by the men who often were erased from our stories.

The era of closed adoptions has largely come to an end, and those that can testify to the experience are dying off. While closed adoptions may be on the decline, some of the practices – convincing women they are unworthy to parent, signing legal documents without informed consent or legal representation, for instance - are not.

This is an ambitious project, and it must be done now and by you. Those who relinquished infants – mothers and fathers – are the only ones who can and will tell the story.

Do You Remember?

I relinquished my baby in 1966, at the height of the Baby Scoop Era. An essential part of my healing is revisiting those days to understand who I was and why I made the decisions I did.

I graduated from high school in June 1965. Our tinny transistor radios played tunes of male domination: “I Will Follow Him,” “Leader of the Pack,” and “It’s My Party and I’ll Cry If I Want To.”

Girls could aspire to be a nurse, secretary, or teacher, until they were married, had children, and became homemakers. Military recruiters were welcome at school with promises of adventure and Hawaii. Vietnam was not mentioned. Only boys were welcome. Sex education was abstinence.

In Massachusetts, where I attended college, contraception for unmarried women and abortion was illegal. No one in my family had ever attended college. I was white, middle-class, suburban, Irish, and Catholic. Unwed mothers were stereotyped as reckless and morally suspect.

Anonymous

In 1966 I had consensual sexual intercourse when I was unmarried. I was delivered of a daughter and signed papers of relinquishment to the Catholic Home Society of New York. These were my actions, and I am responsible for them and all the consequences.

Bob Dylan told us, “The Times They Are A-Changin’.” Social protest against the Vietnam War, the establishment, and women’s rights dominated the headlines in 1968. Food stamps were made available in all states. Contraception bans were overturned in the courts, culminating in Roe v. Wade in 1973. The Equal Rights Amendment was introduced in Congress in 1971.

Our stereos blasted a new soundtrack: “I Am Woman, Hear Me Roar!” “Go Where You Want to Go, Do What You Want to Do,” and “Respect.” I never became a nurse, teacher, or secretary. Instead, I graduated cum laude with a major in history and a minor in philosophy, then built a long, successful career in information technology and insurance management.

Eileen McQuade · July 20, 2026
